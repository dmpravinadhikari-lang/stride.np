**The F-1 interview at the US Embassy in Kathmandu usually lasts two to five minutes, and the officer is deciding one thing: whether you are a genuine student who will leave the United States afterwards.** Almost every refusal is issued under section 214(b), which means you did not convince them of that.

Four minutes is not enough time to explain yourself. It is only enough time to be believed or not. That changes how you should prepare.

## Before the interview: the fees and the form

| Item | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SEVIS I-901 fee | **USD 350** | Pay at least three business days before the interview |
| DS-160 application fee | **USD 185** | Separate payment |

You will also need your I-20 from the institution, your DS-160 confirmation, your passport and your appointment letter.

**One rule that catches people out:** since June 2025, F visa applicants must set their social media accounts to public before the interview, and list every handle used in the past five years on the DS-160. Omitting an account you have used is a far worse problem than anything likely to be on it.

## What officers actually ask

The questions are not exotic. They fall into four groups, and the officer is testing something different with each.

### Why this university, and why this course

- Why did you choose this university?
- How many universities did you apply to? Which ones?
- Why this course and not the equivalent in Nepal?

**What they are testing:** whether a person chose this, or an agent did.

A strong answer names something only that institution has, a specific programme structure, a named faculty member, a lab, a placement semester. A weak answer praises American education in general. If your reason for the university would apply equally to two hundred others, it is not a reason.

### Who is paying, and can you prove it

- Who is sponsoring you?
- What does your sponsor do?
- What is their annual income?
- How much is your first year going to cost?

**What they are testing:** whether the funding is real and whether you personally know your own file.

This is where Nepali applicants most often lose the interview, and rarely because the money is not there. They cannot say the numbers. An applicant who answers "my father has a good business and will manage everything" has told the officer that they have never looked at their own documents.

Know four figures cold, in your own words:

1. Total first-year cost
2. Your sponsor's declared annual income
3. Any loan amount and what secures it
4. What is in the bank now

### What happens after you graduate

- What will you do after your degree?
- Do you have family in the United States?
- Why come back to Nepal?

**What they are testing:** non-immigrant intent.

"I will serve my country" appears in an enormous share of refused interviews. It says nothing. Name a sector, name the kind of employer, and give a reason the qualification matters *in Nepal specifically*. If you have a family business to return to, or land, or a parent you are responsible for, say so plainly.

### Your background

- What did you study before? What were your marks?
- What have you been doing since?
- Have you taken IELTS or TOEFL? What did you score?

**What they are testing:** consistency with the file in front of them, and gaps.

An unexplained gap year is read as something concealed. A gap you explain in one sentence, worked here, cared for a parent, retook a test, is not an issue.

## Why short answers win

Officers are not looking for completeness. They are pattern-matching, at speed, against thousands of applicants.

Two habits sink otherwise good candidates:

**Over-explaining.** You answer the question, then keep talking, and volunteer something that opens a line of questioning you did not want. Answer, then stop.

**The rehearsed paragraph.** A fluent 40-second speech in response to "why this university?" reads as coached, especially when the next answer is delivered in halting English. Know the substance; let the words come out differently each time.

## A worked example

The same true facts, answered two ways.

> **Question:** Who is paying for your studies, and can you prove it?

**The answer that fails:** *"My father will sponsor me. He has a good business and our financial condition is strong, so there will be no problem."*

No number anywhere. "Good business" and "no problem" are what an officer hears from an applicant who has never opened their own bank file. It invites three more questions.

**The answer that passes:** *"My father. He runs a registered construction supply business in Bharatpur and declared NPR 42 lakh last year. I have his tax clearance and audited statements. With an education loan of NPR 35 lakh against our land, that covers my first-year cost of USD 38,000."*

Four checkable facts in twenty seconds. The officer stops digging because there is nothing left to find.
