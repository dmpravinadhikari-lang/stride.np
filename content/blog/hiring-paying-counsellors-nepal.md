**The expensive part of losing a counsellor is not recruiting the next one.** It is the forty students whose history was in that person's head, whose parents trusted that person specifically, and whose next steps nobody can now reconstruct.

That risk is built or avoided on the first day of employment, not the last.

## Hire for the willingness to lose a sale

Everything else can be taught. Destination requirements change every year anyway, so the counsellor who has memorised this year's rules has a shorter advantage than they think.

What cannot be taught quickly is the instinct to tell a student their plan is wrong. A student with weak finances who wants a destination with a heavy funds requirement can be sold that application. They will pay, they will be refused, and you will carry the refusal in your record with that institution and in what that family says about you for years.

In the interview, ask for a time they talked someone out of something. If the answer is thin, keep looking.

## Structure the roles before you need them

Most offices grow as an undifferentiated group of people who all do everything, and then discover at around six or seven staff that nobody owns anything.

A workable shape early on:

- **Front desk or reception.** Takes the walk in, records the enquiry properly, books the counselling. Often the most underrated role, because everything downstream depends on the enquiry being captured at all.
- **Counsellors.** Own students by destination or by stage, not by whoever happened to greet them.
- **Documentation.** Once you are placing regularly, the checking of files is specialist work and doing it between counselling sessions is how errors reach an embassy.
- **Test preparation.** Usually separate people on a separate rhythm.
- **Accounts and compliance.** Part time long before it is full time, and needed earlier than owners expect.

You do not need six people. You need to know which of these hats a person is wearing when they sit down, because "everyone does everything" is the arrangement under which a missed intake deadline belongs to nobody.

## Pay: the timing problem

Commission from an institution may arrive a year after the student was placed. A counsellor cannot wait a year, so the pay structure has to bridge a gap the business genuinely has.

What tends to work: a salary that a person can live on, plus an incentive paid at a milestone that happens while the student is still in front of you. Visa granted is a common choice and a sound one. It is late enough to mean something and early enough to feel connected to the work.

Two failure modes to avoid. **Paying on enrolment or offer** rewards volume and quietly encourages weak applications. **Paying only when the institution pays you** makes the counsellor a creditor of the business, and good ones will not accept it twice.

Whatever you choose, write it down and calculate it the same way every month. Incentive schemes that are recalculated at the owner's discretion are the most reliable way to lose the person you most wanted to keep.

## Keep commission figures away from the counselling desk

A counsellor who knows which institution pays the most has been given a reason to recommend it, and students are better at sensing that than most owners believe.

Keep the rate card with whoever manages partnerships and accounts. The counsellor's job is to place the student correctly; the business's job is to make sure the correct placements are also commercially sensible. Merging those two jobs in one person's head is how an office ends up with a refusal rate it cannot explain.

## The handover problem, solved on day one

Ask this about each counsellor: if they resigned tomorrow, could someone else open a student's file and know what was promised, what was sent, what is outstanding and when the next action is due?

If the answer is no, the office does not employ that counsellor so much as depend on them.

The fix is not a contract clause. It is that every student's history lives in one shared place, updated as the work happens rather than written up later. Then a resignation is an inconvenience rather than an emergency, and a counsellor's holiday stops being something the office dreads.

There is a second benefit that owners notice within a month: you can finally see who is carrying too much. The counsellor with ninety live files is not slacking, they are drowning, and they will resign before they tell you. That is visible in a shared system and invisible in a set of private notebooks.
