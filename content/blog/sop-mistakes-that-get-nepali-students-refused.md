**Almost every refused statement from Nepal fails for the same reason: it is full of sentences that could have been written by anyone.** An assessor reading their four-hundredth statement of the season is not looking for beautiful English. They are looking for facts that can be checked, and for a story that agrees with the rest of your file.

Here are the lines that come up again and again in statements that do not work, why each one is read as a warning sign, and what to write instead.

## 1. "I want to serve my country"

This appears in an enormous share of refused applications. It is almost certainly true, and it is completely uninformative.

**Why it fails:** it tells the assessor nothing about you, and everyone writes it.

**Write instead:** name a sector, a kind of employer, and the reason the qualification matters *in Nepal specifically*.

> *"Nepal's banks are being pushed into serious security compliance for the first time, and there are very few people here who have worked with these standards. My former employer has already indicated interest in a senior network security role."*

## 2. "The university has world-class facilities and an excellent reputation"

**Why it fails:** it applies equally to every university on earth, which means you have not explained choosing this one. Assessors read this as a sentence written by someone who did not choose, an agent did.

**Write instead:** something only that institution offers.

> *"I chose Wollongong for the industry placement semester and for the network security group's published work on low-bandwidth authentication, which is exactly the problem I hit building a patient-records system in Chitwan."*

## 3. "My father will bear all my expenses"

**Why it fails:** there is no number in it. Financial vagueness is the single most common weakness we see in statements from Nepal, and it is the easiest to fix.

**Write instead:** the figures, and where they come from.

> *"My father runs a registered construction supply business in Bharatpur and declared NPR 42 lakh last year, supported by three years of tax clearance. We have an education loan of NPR 35 lakh sanctioned against family land valued at NPR 1.1 crore. Together these cover my first-year tuition of AUD 34,000 and living costs."*

## 4. "Since childhood I have dreamed of becoming an engineer"

**Why it fails:** it is unverifiable, it is used constantly, and it wastes your opening. The paragraph most likely to be read closely.

**Write instead:** start at the concrete moment that actually points at this course.

> *"During my BSc CSIT I spent two semesters building a patient-records system for a clinic in Chitwan. What defeated us was not the code. It was securing data across unreliable connections."*

## 5. Silence about the study gap

**Why it fails:** the assessor has your transcripts. They can see the two years. A gap you did not mention is a gap you appear to be hiding, and that costs you more than the gap itself.

**Write instead:** name it, account for it, move on. Two sentences.

> *"The year after my bachelors was not idle: I worked as a junior support engineer at a Kathmandu ISP, handling routing escalations for business customers. That work is the reason I am applying for the security stream rather than general IT."*

## 6. A course that does not follow from your past, unexplained

A BBA graduate applying for a hospitality diploma. An engineer applying for business analytics. These are perfectly legitimate, and they need a paragraph.

**Why it fails when unexplained:** it looks like the course was chosen for admission odds or visa odds rather than for study.

**Write instead:** own the change and give it a reason rooted in what you have actually done.

## 7. Numbers that contradict your documents

Your statement says your sponsor earns NPR 50 lakh. The tax clearance says 42. You have now created a credibility problem out of nothing.

**Why it fails:** an inconsistency across a file is treated far more seriously than a modest figure. It suggests the file was assembled to persuade rather than to describe.

**Write instead:** the number on the document. Every time. Check your statement against your papers line by line before you submit. This is the cheapest hour you will spend.

## 8. Language that is not yours

If your statement reads like a policy paper and your visa interview does not, the mismatch is obvious and damaging, particularly for the UK, where the credibility interview and the statement are assessed together.

**Why it fails:** assessors compare. A statement well beyond the applicant's demonstrated English is a flag, and it is one they act on.

**Write instead:** plain sentences you could say out loud.

## The test to apply to every paragraph

Ask of each sentence: **could this appear, word for word, in another Nepali student's statement?**

If yes, it is doing nothing for you. Replace it with something only you could have written, a project, a place, a person, a number, a date.

That is the whole discipline. It is more work than writing pleasant generalities, and it is why the statements that work read plainer than students expect.

## A note on AI-written statements

You can have a machine write you a whole statement in seconds. Understand what you are taking on if you do.

Many universities now screen for machine-written text, and a flagged statement can be treated as academic misconduct before you have enrolled, some institutions withdraw offers over it. Beyond detection, generated text tends to share a rhythm and vocabulary that experienced readers recognise, which puts you straight back into problem 1 above.

The sensible use is as an interrogator, not an author: let it question your draft, point at the vague sentences, and tell you which paragraph an assessor will stop at. Then write it yourself.

## Before you submit

Read your statement beside your documents and check every figure matches. Then read it out loud, if you would not say a sentence to an officer's face, do not put it in writing.
